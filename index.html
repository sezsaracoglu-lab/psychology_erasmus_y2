A note before you begin

Sezen — the lecture that should change how you think about your own past

Lecture 1 left us at the door of long-term memory: attention decides what gets in. This lecture walks through that door and asks the harder question. Once something is in — is it safe?

It is not. And the reason is not that memory is bad at its job. It is that memory is doing a different job than we assume. We treat remembering as replay: the event was recorded, and recall means pressing play. Every finding in this lecture says remembering is reconstruction — you rebuild the episode fresh each time, out of whatever material is available, and material that arrived long after the event is just as available as the event itself.

The lecture is organised as an argument you can reconstruct from four moves. It locates the kind of memory at issue (episodic — the vulnerable one). It splits that into three processes. It shows a bias attacking each process in turn. And it finishes by asking how far the damage can go — whether an entire event can be installed in someone who was never there.

How to read this platform. Each lesson is one diagram plus five bullets and a memory hook. The diagram is the lesson; the bullets are captions. Lesson 9 is the centrepiece and Lesson 10 is the figure most likely to appear in your exam — spend your time there.

One last thing. This material is unusually easy to over-claim about, and the tutorials will reward precision. I have flagged the two live scientific caveats in amber rather than smoothing them over.

LESSON 01Why this lecture exists

The Steve Titus Problem

A witness who is certain, an innocent man, and no one lying. How is that possible?

  1. In 1980 Steve Titus was picked up near Seattle because his beard and his car loosely matched a rape victim's description. He had no connection to the crime.
  2. From a photo montage she said he "looked the most like" the attacker. By the trial she was certain: "That's the man." He was convicted.
  3. A journalist later found the real assailant. Titus was cleared — but he had lost his job and his fiancée, and died of a stress-related heart attack at 35.
  4. Nobody lied. The witness's memory genuinely changed between the montage and the trial. That is the phenomenon this whole lecture is about.
  5. Scale: more than 75,000 people a year in the US are charged on the basis of eyewitness testimony. Researchers warned from the 1970s; the courts listened only in the 1990s, when DNA evidence made wrongful convictions provable — the work of the Innocence Project.
🔑
Memory hook
The confidence went up. The evidence never moved.
LESSON 02Where it lives · zooming into Lecture 1's third box

Inside Long-Term Memory

"Long-term memory" is not one thing. Which part of it is the one that goes wrong?

  1. Declarative memory = memory for facts and events — things you can declare, put into words. It divides into semantic and episodic.
  2. Procedural memory = memory for skills. Three properties: automatic through practice, no intentional recall, and resistant to forgetting. You cannot say how you balance on a bike — and you also cannot lose it.
  3. Semantic memory = general knowledge, stripped of the occasion on which you learned it.
  4. Episodic memory = memory for events you personally experienced.
  5. The whole lecture lives in one leaf of this tree. Semantic and procedural memory are resistant; episodic memory is not. Nobody worries about a witness misremembering what a car is.
🎓Exam type "which memory system?" — 8 × 5 = 40 → semantic (a fact, no occasion attached). The maths test where you learned it → episodic. Doing arithmetic automatically → procedural.
🔑
Memory hook
Encyclopaedia, diary, and the hands that already know the piano. Only the diary can be edited.
LESSON 03Where it lives · the distinction that sets up everything

Semantic vs. Episodic — and the Confidence Trap

Why is the kind of memory that feels the most real also the one you should trust the least?

  1. Semantic answers what. Episodic answers when and where. That is the fastest way to sort an exam example.
  2. The phrasing test: semantic memories come out as "I know…", episodic ones as "I remember…".
  3. Only episodic memory carries a recollective experience — the sense of mentally reliving the moment. Semantic memory has none: you do not relive learning the capital of France.
  4. Semantic memory is resistant to forgetting; episodic memory is vulnerable.
  5. Put 3 and 4 together and you have the trap: the system that produces the strongest feeling of certainty is the least reliable one. Vividness is not evidence — and a jury has no way to tell the difference.
🔑
Memory hook
"I know" is dull and dependable. "I remember" is vivid and unreliable. Confidence is a feeling, not a measurement.
LESSON 04How it works · the skeleton of the whole lecture

The Three Processes

Remembering is not one act. How many separate places are there for it to go wrong?

  1. Encoding — the initial learning of information: perceiving it and relating it to past knowledge.
  2. Storage — maintaining information over time.
  3. Retrieval — accessing information when it is needed.
  4. Each can be biased, independently. This is the structural insight the lecture is built on: once you see three separable processes, you know in advance there must be three families of error — and that a fix aimed at one will not touch the others.
  5. Attention (Lecture 1) sits upstream of encoding. What the filter rejects never reaches step 1, so it cannot be recovered by any amount of careful questioning at step 3.
🔑
Memory hook
Watching, filing, fetching. A tape can only fail while watching. A memory can fail at all three — and the filing cabinet isn't locked.
LESSON 05How it works · process 1 up close

Encoding: Continuous, Selective, Recoding

Perceiving is not recording. What does the mind actually do to an experience on the way in?

  1. Encoding is continuous: it runs all the time, without any decision on your part. You do not choose to become a witness.
  2. Encoding is selective, and the selection is attention — the direct inheritance from Lecture 1. What fails the filter never gets encoded at all.
  3. Two things push material through the filter: distinctiveness (unusual things get in) and emotional content.
  4. Flashbulb memories are the vivid, confidently held memories of shocking events that the emotion route produces. Note the pattern from Lesson 3 repeating: high vividness, high confidence — and no guarantee of accuracy.
  5. Recoding converts information into a form that makes sense to you. It is what makes memory possible and the first point at which interpretation starts standing in for observation.
🔑
Memory hook
The camera is always running, it points where attention points, and it saves the gist rather than the pixels.
LESSON 06How it works · process 2 up close

Storage: Engram, Consolidation, Decay

A memory is not finished when the event ends. What happens to it in the dark?

  1. Experiences leave memory traces, or engrams — the physical residue of having lived through something.
  2. Consolidation is the neural change that occurs after learning, over time, to imprint the engram. A memory is not finished when the event ends; it is still setting, like concrete.
  3. Decay is the fading of traces with the passage of time.
  4. Read the curve as a tug-of-war: consolidation strengthens the trace for a while, then decay slowly wins. The trace is at its most fragile at both ends — before it has set, and long after.
  5. Hold on to that shape. In Lesson 10 you will see what happens when misleading information arrives while the trace is on the way down: the weaker the original, the more the newcomer wins.
🔑
Memory hook
The engram is wet concrete. Consolidation is it setting. Decay is the weather. And anything pressed into it while wet stays there.
LESSON 07How it breaks · encoding bias I · Clifford & Scott (1978)

Characteristics of the Observed

Some events are harder to remember than others. Is it the horror — or the strangeness?

  1. Clifford & Scott (1978) — a between-subjects manipulation of the same film: officers either persuade a third person to give up information, or extract it with violence. A distractor task, then an unexpected recall test.
  2. Recall was poorer in the violent condition — and poorer for the entirety of the film, not just the violent stretch. Whatever the violence did, it did not stay local.
  3. Weapon focus = the presence of a weapon causes eyewitnesses to fail to recall details about the assailant and the environment.
  4. Eye-tracking shows the mechanism directly: more fixations on the gun than the cheque, and longer ones. Steblay's meta-analysis puts the cost at d ≈ .13 for identification and d ≈ .55 for describing the perpetrator.
  5. The arousal and unusualness accounts make different predictions, and that is what makes them testable: a gun at a shooting range is threatening but expected. The effect shrinks — which is trouble for arousal alone.
🔑
Memory hook
The witness stared at the barrel, not the face. You cannot recall what you never encoded.
LESSON 08How it breaks · encoding bias II · Hastorf & Cantril (1954)

Characteristics of the Observer

One film, two universities, two completely different games. Who was right?

  1. A famously bad-tempered 1951 Dartmouth–Princeton football game left Princeton's star with a broken nose and a Dartmouth player with a broken leg. Both campuses blamed the other side.
  2. The researchers showed the same game film to students at both colleges and asked them to count the infractions they saw and rate each as mild or flagrant. Same film, same instructions.
  3. Dartmouth students saw an even game. Princeton students saw the Dartmouth team commit 9.8 infractions against their own team's 4.2.
  4. The authors' conclusion: there is no such thing as "the game" existing out there to be observed. It exists for each person only through what is significant to them.
  5. The lecture's label for this is confirmation bias: expectations bias perception, and perception is what gets encoded. Neither group is lying — and neither can see the tint on its own lens.
🔑
Memory hook
Lesson 7 is the camera pointing at the wrong thing. Lesson 8 is the camera having a favourite team.
LESSON 09How it breaks · storage bias · ⭐ the centrepiece

The Loftus Ladder: Strengthen → Distort → Create

Information that arrives after the event. How much damage can a single word do?

  1. Level 1 — STRENGTHEN (Loftus, 1975). Question 1 either mentioned the stop sign the car really ran, or said "turned right". Later, 53% vs 35% said they had seen a stop sign. Merely mentioning something makes it more likely to be remembered. Note: the information was consistent — it was true.
  2. Level 2 — DISTORT (Loftus, 1977). A green car passed the scene; half the participants were later asked about the "blue car". Choosing the colour from a wheel afterwards, they shifted toward blue — many landing on blue-green intermediates. The lecture calls these compromise memories.
  3. Level 3 — CREATE (Loftus, Miller & Burns, 1978). A stop sign or a yield sign; the later question named the other one. Forced choice between the two slides: 75% correct after consistent information, 41% after misleading information.
  4. The ladder is the argument. Consistent information reinforces; inconsistent information first bends and then invents.
  5. And it is not a laboratory artefact. In a field demonstration (Loftus, 1979), bystanders to a staged theft at a train station later told a supposed insurance agent they had seen the tape recorder — describing it in detail. There had never been one in the bag.
🔑
Memory hook
Reinforce the wire · bend the wire · run a whole new wire. And they are all the same colour afterwards.
LESSON 10How it breaks · the figure most likely to be examined

The Misinformation Curve

Misleading information does damage. Does it matter when it arrives?

  1. Three kinds of post-event information — consistent (matches the event), neutral (mentions nothing), inconsistent (names the wrong sign) — tested across five retention intervals.
  2. The ordering never changes: consistent above neutral above inconsistent. That alone answers most exam questions on this figure.
  3. The gap widens. Immediately after the film all three sit near 80–86%. By one week, consistent is around 66% while inconsistent has fallen to about 21%.
  4. Why? The original memory decays (Lesson 6) while the misinformation stays fresh. So the later the misleading question arrives, the more damage it does.
  5. This is the post-event misinformation effect, an instance of retroactive interference. Note the legal implication: a system that interviews witnesses repeatedly over months is running the worst possible protocol.
🎓The recommendation the lecturer asks you to derive. Interview witnesses as early as possible; keep other people's accounts out of the room; ask open questions rather than ones that presuppose facts; and do not treat a confident witness as an accurate one.
🔑
Memory hook
The truth fades. The lie doesn't. So the longer you wait to ask, the more the lie wins.
LESSON 11How it breaks · theory comparison

Why Misinformation Works: Three Explanations

Two of the three are struck through on the slide. Do you know what killed them?

  1. (a) Social pressure — rejected. The idea that participants simply comply with what the experimenter implies. Rewards and warnings reduce the effect but do not remove it, and misled participants respond faster when choosing the misinformation — the opposite of deliberate compliance.
  2. (b) Memory replacement theory — rejected. The idea that the new detail overwrites and destroys the old one.
  3. What killed it: the modified test. McCloskey and Zaragoza removed the misleading item from the answer options, so participants chose between the original and a novel item. Standard test: 72% vs 37%. Modified test: 75% vs 72%. If the original had been destroyed it could not perform that well.
  4. (c) Memory coexistence theory — accepted. Both representations survive side by side; the error is at retrieval.
  5. Source monitoring is the process of attributing a memory to its origin — did I see this, hear it, read it, imagine it? Source is not stored as a label; it is inferred at retrieval from the qualitative feel of the memory. That is why a vividly imagined detail can pass as a witnessed one.
🔑
Memory hook
Nothing was deleted. Everything was filed — with the labels torn off.
LESSON 12How it breaks · retrieval bias

Leading Questions & the Circumstances of Recall

The memory may be intact. Can the way you ask for it still change the answer?

  1. Leading question = a question whose wording presupposes or suggests part of the answer. "Smashed" versus "contacted" moved the estimated speed by more than 10 mph — for the same film.
  2. The follow-up shows it is memory, not politeness. A week later, 32% of the "smashed" group reported broken glass that was never in the film, against 14% ("hit") and 12% (control).
  3. Godden & Baddeley (1975) took 18 divers and crossed learning environment with recall environment. Matched conditions — land/land 38%, water/water 32% — beat mismatched ones (24%, 23%) by roughly 40%.
  4. Context-dependent memory: recall is best when the retrieval context reinstates the encoding context. The environment acts as a retrieval cue.
  5. Two opposite lessons, one point. A leading question hands you part of the answer; the right context hands you back the memory. What you get out depends on the conditions of asking, not only on what went in.
🎓Two honest caveats worth having. The published mean for "contacted" is 31.8 mph, not the 30.5 on the slide — the ordering, which is what gets examined, is identical. And the diving result failed a preregistered replication in 2021; the principle of context-dependent memory rests on a much wider evidence base than that one study.
🔑
Memory hook
"Smashed" put glass on the road that was never there. And the divers had the words all along — they just needed the water back.
LESSON 13The limit case · and the exam toolkit

Implanting a Whole Memory

Everything so far edited a memory that existed. Can you install one that never did?

  1. Piaget (1962) could vividly "see" being nearly kidnapped from his pram. His nurse later confessed she had invented the whole thing. He concluded he had heard the story as a child and "projected it into the past in the form of a visual memory" — and it stayed vivid even after he knew it was false.
  2. Loftus & Pickrell (1995) — "lost in the mall". Of 24 adults given three real childhood events and one fabricated one, 25% came to remember the event that never happened, against 68% recall for the true events.
  3. Braun et al. (2002) — an advertisement did it commercially: 16% later recalled meeting Bugs Bunny at Disneyland, which is impossible.
  4. The engine is always the same: you cannot tell from the inside where a memory came from. Hearing about it, imagining it, seeing it advertised and living through it leave broadly similar traces, and none of them comes with a receipt.
  5. The exam rule: ask when the manipulation happened. During the event → encoding. In the gap → storage. At the moment of asking → retrieval.
⚠️State this one carefully. The lost-in-the-mall paradigm has been criticised — the effect depends on the event being plausible, so implanting a plausible memory does not license claims about implausible ones. A preregistered replication (2023, N = 123) did find the effect at 35%, so the phenomenon is real; what stays contested is how far it generalises.
🔑
Memory hook
Levels 1–3 vandalised a house that existed. This one builds a house on an empty plot — and the owner moves in.
